The prospect of a crypto monoculture

By Jonathan Corbet
March 30, 2016
There is a constant tension in the free-software world between consolidation and diversity. When we focus on a single program or algorithm, the entire force of the community is directed toward improving that one thing, often with impressive results. But a diverse set of solutions has its own advantages, including fewer single points of failure, the ability to pursue a wider range of interesting ideas, and increased resistance to deliberate attacks. This tension may soon come to the fore in the realm of cryptographic algorithms, according to one researcher in the field.

Peter Gutmann is a well-known participant in encryption-oriented discussions; he is also the author of Engineering Security [814-page PDF]. He recently stirred up some discussion with a posting titled On the impending crypto monoculture. Therein, he states, a number of IETF groups are currently working on a set of redesigned cryptographic standards with an interesting common theme:

A major feature of these changes includes the dropping of traditional encryption algorithms and mechanisms like RSA, DH, ECDH/ECDSA, SHA-2, and AES, for a completely different set of mechanisms, including Curve25519 (designed by Dan Bernstein et al), EdDSA (Bernstein and colleagues), Poly1305 (Bernstein again) and ChaCha20 (by, you guessed it, Bernstein).

For added fun, Daniel is also the author of the reference implementations for these algorithms. So, Peter says, we are heading toward "a never-before-seen crypto monoculture in which it's possible that the entire algorithm suite used by a security protocol, and the entire implementation of that suite, all originate from one person." He doesn't really get into the consequences of this situation; it is implicitly understood to be something other than a good outcome.

The posting does go into considerable detail on how this situation came about; it is worth reading in its entirety. In short: there is a strong desire to leave current algorithms and implementations behind because they have been proved, repeatedly, to be brittle and failure-prone. "Anyone who works with crypto on the Internet has had to endure 15-20 years of constant breakage of the crypto they use, both of the algorithms and mechanisms and of the implementations." He mentions Google in particular, since Google is a major participant in the IETF standards process; the company has gotten tired of having to deal with a new attack every few months and would like to transition to something more robust.

While "fanboyism" is a part of why Daniel's algorithms and code are on the ascendant, Peter says, there is more to it than that. In short, the alternatives are either brittle and failure-prone themselves, or they are patent-encumbered and unusable. There is no effective competition to Daniel's work in the real world. Or, to put it a bit more graphically:

Consider being lost in an endless desert. If you see an oasis in the distance, you head towards it even if the water is brackish and has camel dung floating in it. Bernstein et al are the oasis (or perhaps the mirage of an oasis), in an endless desert of cryptosystems and implementations of cryptosystems that keep breaking.

As a result, he says, the move toward a monoculture isn't really a vote for Daniel and his work; it's a vote against everything else out there.

Is this a problem?

Peter does not get into why a cryptographic monoculture would be a problem, perhaps because some of the disadvantages are fairly clear to see. If Daniel proves, over time, to have some sort of blind spot, all of his work, at either the algorithmic or implementation level, could prove to be affected. If he turns out to have been in the pay of some nation's security apparatus, the consequences could even be worse — though, in this regard, at least, Daniel somehow seems like a safer bet than many. Either way, some sort of systemic flaw in Daniel's work could lead to a situation where there are no safe algorithms to fall back on.

On the other hand, a wide range of unrelated algorithms has not necessarily proved to be safer. They lead to a dispersal of effort and review time so that, seemingly, none of them reach a level of maturity that allows them to be trusted.

There are examples both ways. Of the things that keep system administrators awake at night, a remotely exploitable OpenSSH bug must be near the top of the list. For all practical purposes, OpenSSH is a monoculture; it is, by far, the dominant SSH implementation. A vulnerability in OpenSSH could lead to the compromise of vast numbers of systems. Such vulnerabilities have been rare, though, as the result of a high-quality implementation and a great deal of code review (though the recent "roaming vulnerability" makes one wonder what else the reviewers might have missed). As a result, one does not often hear people worrying about the OpenSSH monoculture.

On the other hand, OpenSSL is the focus of more concern; there have been a few times when it might have been nicer to have a more diverse set of widely deployed TLS implementations in use. Indeed, there are now a number of projects working in an attempt to provide that diversity. One can argue about that the problems with OpenSSL stem from within the project itself, but its near-monoculture status ensured that those problems affected a large portion of the net as a whole.

Given that a new set of cryptographic standards could end up underpinning security across the net, it is natural, and wise, to be worried about building the whole thing on the work of one person. There is simply too much at stake to take the risk of a widespread vulnerability with no fallback available. One hopes that Peter's warning will be taken seriously — and that, somehow, an alternative to a monoculture will emerge from somewhere.

It isn't that hard. An obvious counter example to the claim that the cascaded encryption is as strong as the stronger encryption is encoding the message first into unary, and then xor'ing with a one time pad. Using one time pads is fairly secure but you don't need to break that to know that "Fxay" means 4 and "gYs" means 3. Assuming that we aren't relying on security by obscurity and encrypting is intended to be easier that decrypting with out the key, and the keys are independent then:

0) We aren't using rot13
1) Cascading encryption is at least as strong as the inner cypher (not necessarily the weakest)
2) Cascading encryption with commutative cyphers is as at least as hard as the strongest cypher.

More details at: http://www.isiweb.ee.ethz.ch/archive/massey_pub/pdf/BI434...
The paper is relatively light reading, free from heavy mathematical machinery.

Double-crypt?

Posted Mar 31, 2016 14:10 UTC (Thu) by Beolach (guest, #77384) [Link] (6 responses)

It depends on the crypto algorithms & implementations. Triple-DES is well known to be better than DES. Double-ROT13 is well known to be identical to plain-text.

But as a general rule, I'd be quite skeptical of anything claiming double-cypt w/ different algorithms adds additional security. Your stated "double-encryption is as strong as the stronger of the two encryptions" is theoretically possible, but that's the best-case scenario. Having a strong encryption combined w/ a weaker encryption will NEVER be stronger than the strong encryption on its own - and depending on the algorithms & implementations the strong+weak could be weaker than strong on its own.

Looking only at strength (i.e. ignoring costs & usability), the best encryption possible is a truly random one-time-pad. But combining an OTP w/ any other crypto-system will never be stronger: mathematically speaking, it will just be same as using a different OTP. And the new, different OTP is likely to not be truly random, and therefore likely to be weaker. This is what k3ninho was describing - note that he didn't say "double encryption is as weak as the weaker of the two encryptions" (your paraphrase), he said "the weakest crypto is the weakest link and limits the strength of the chain". Which is true enough, for a poor metaphor of a chain. If an attacker has enough strength to break the strongest link in the chain, then it doesn't matter that there are also weaker links in the chain. And having more links might give the attacker a better grip or leverage & improve their odds of breaking that strong link as opposed to if the strong link was on its own. Yeah, a physical chain is a poor metaphor to modern encryption.

As far, as I know, AES should become stronger using larger keys. But it becomes less performant, too. There is always a conflict between performance and strength.

Chaining up two 128-bit AES is obviously a bad idea. The strength should roughly double* compared to just one 128-bit AES, whereas a 256-bit AES is believed to be many times stronger than 128-bit AES. The known cracking algorithms are still exponential in the bit-length. If AES should be completely broken in the future, chaining up two AES will not help much, but combining AES with some different cipher might help, if the other cipher is not broken.

*actually it can more than double, because the outer AES will see merely garbage while only one AES would directly see the secret, which usually is not completely random.

Suppose we encrypt f(g(x,key_2),key_1). Of course this is not much better than the identity function. But in the composition scenario, it is even worse, as the attacker knows that the input to f consists of a string, where each letter occurs twice. Btw. such a repetition was a big problem for the Enigma machine. Using such a bad inner function might considerably lower the overall strength.

Judging how a weak (but not completely stupid) inner encryption might affect the overall strength is very hard. Up to know, I do not even know any proof that the commonly used ciphers (other than OTP) are actually much harder to break than the identity function. Definitely, we have no proof that the required time is superpolynomial (this would imply P!=NP), but I do not think that there is even a proof that it is more than linear. This makes mathematical reasoning about crypto ciphers a bit problematic.

I would not believe that combination of crypto algorithm will weaken the overall strength much below the strength of the strongest cipher. To achieve this, knowing that the input y is actually y=g(x,key_2) for some secret x and some key key_2 has to expose more information than knowing that y=x for some secret x. This seems to be very unlikely, unless we explicitly use a g that will accomplish just that. In practice you will know much more about the secret x (e.g., you know that it is a HTTP session) than you know about y=g(x,key_2). But of course, I cannot give any proof.

To get to some other argument: Adding another outer encryption layer can never decrease the strength of the overall encryption below the strength of the inner layer. Any outer encryption, that I add to my already encrypted message could also be done by the person trying to crack the message. If someone could crack WEP(TLS(x)) faster than TLS(x), than the person can always add the WEP layer before trying to crack TLS(x).

If anything could be learned from this, than that you should put the functions you believe to be stronger to the inside.

Double-crypt?

Posted Apr 2, 2016 18:13 UTC (Sat) by nybble41 (subscriber, #55106) [Link] (2 responses)

> If anything could be learned from this, than that you should put the functions you believe to be stronger to the inside.

More generally, you should avoid giving potential attackers a known plaintext for cryptoanalysis. Even a strong encryption algorithm could have patterns in the output which are not a problem for the algorithm itself but would constitute a known plaintext for any outer encryption layer. For example, AES wouldn't get any weaker just because you doubled each byte in the ciphertext, but if you encrypted the result it could give the attacker extra information to use in breaking the outer layer if the outer algorithm is not resistant to known-plaintext attacks.

I would argue that none of this actually makes f(g(x)) any weaker than either g(x) or f(y), for arbitrary plaintexts x and y. If f is vulnerable to a known-plaintext attack depending on the output of g(x), then f(g(x)) _may_ be easier to break than f(x), but it would depend on x: the same kinds of patterns found in g(x) could have been present in x to begin with. The strength of f(y) for arbitrary y necessarily includes the case where y is the output from g(x) for some plaintext x. The weakness is thus inherent in f itself, not just when it's composed with g.

It implies P!=NP if the problem of breaking the encryption is contained in NP. Then we would have found some problem in NP that is not contained in P. The encryption and decryption problem with known keys is usually in P, as otherwise cryptography would not be performant enough. The problem of decrypting without a key is in NP by the following algorithm:

1. Guess a key.
2. Use the polynomial time decryption routine.
3. Test whether the output is valid.

Whether the problem is NP-hard does not matter. Btw., the third step is where OTP is winning. It is impossible to tell whether the decryption got the correct output. With other ciphers, it is highly unlikely to get a sensible output with a wrong key.

If we are very strcit, then yes of course, it matters. And we are a bit leaving the complexity theory ang going into information theory. How many informations are in the encrypted text? Is it possible to compute the clear text (with unlimited computational power). For OTP, I already said, you cannot verify. This is a purely information theoretic argument. The encrypted text contains zero information about the clear text. Only together with the key (which has the same size as the message), you can get anything back.

For the more common ciphers, the encrypted text has a lot of information. After all, it can only have up to 256 bit less information than the plain text (if speaking about AES-256), because adding the information of the key reveals the plain text. This usually allows verification.

To solve the problem with the formal definitions of P and NP, one would usually reformulate the problem a bit to get to a real NP algorithm. There are two sensible directions:

1. Does there exist a key, such that the decrypted clear text message is a valid english text?
2. Does there exist a key, such that the decrypted clear text message is the given clear text message?

The second one is known as "knwon plain-text attack".

Note, that the formal definition of NP only allows yes/no questions. The problem compute a key such that the message is correct english, cannot be contained in NP, as it is no yes/no question. It could be NP-hard, as NP-hard problems do not need to be contained in NP. However, as already noticed by you, we do not really expect these problems to be NP-hard.

Of course, one might ask, whether the reformulated problem would help someone attacking cryptography. For OTP it does certainly not help. In fact for OTP, the reformulated problem is trivial, as we can easily compute for a given message a key, such that the decryption exactly computes the message. For the normal symmetric and asymmetric ciphers however, one would certainly say that the cipher is broken if one of the two problems above could be solved efficiently.
