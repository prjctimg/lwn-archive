The 2026 Maintainers Summit

By Jonathan Corbet
October 12, 2026
The Maintainers Summit is a one-day, invitation-only gathering of about 30 kernel developers; it is held once each year to discuss policy-related issues that are not always amenable to resolution via email. The 2026 event was scheduled for October 8 in Prague, Czechia. Unsurprisingly, the discussion there was dominated by large language models (LLMs) and their effect on the development community, but some time was also found to talk about security bugs and long-term succession planning.

Unlike previous years, the 2026 Summit did not have a well-defined schedule with specific developers identified to lead the discussion on each topic. The conversation was rather more free-flowing, and this writeup will match that structure (or lack thereof). Some minimal reordering has been done in an attempt to provide a more coherent overview.

TL;DR

For those who do not want to plow through all that follows, here is a summary of the most significant conclusions. Please note that this is entirely my interpretation; it is not any sort of official communication from the Summit.

  • Sashiko no longer reports pre-existing bugs found when reviewing patches; the reaction to this change was mixed. Sashiko is appreciated, but some maintainers fear it fuels the rapid tweak-and-resend cycle that new submitters tend to fall into.
  • Linus Torvalds believes that blaming LLMs for the kernel community's problems is wrong, when the problem is LLM users who do not understand how to use them or how the kernel works. A properly trained LLM is a useful tool, but it takes a lot of time to reach that point.
  • Torvalds and others repeatedly said low-quality contributions from new developers should receive low priority, and that the community should stop trying to make contribution easy. Many developers said, repeatedly, that trying to mentor those developers is almost always a waste of time; they do not listen.
  • There is some concern about companies maintaining private kernels with LLM help, but there does not seem to be much to be done about it.
  • Kernel developers should not be required to use LLMs to work in the community, but those who do need to have enough resources available to generate acceptable results.
  • The requirement to add Assisted-by tags to patches created with LLMs will be removed, but maintainers still want to know about how a patch was created.
  • The development community is struggling to keep up with the flood of security patches, and will continue to for some time. Some of the most urgent fixes may bypass maintainers and go straight to Torvalds. Maintainers were cautioned against telling their employers about vulnerabilities before public disclosure happens.
  • The maintainers each briefly presented an aspect of the development process that causes them pain; many talked about the review process, keeping up with the load, and submitters who are not interested in learning.
  • Torvalds said that the community should start thinking more about how leadership succession should work so that there will be a plan in place when, some years from now, he decides to retire.

Sashiko and patch review

The initial topic was automated patch review, and the Sashiko review system in particular. When Sashiko is looking at a specific patch, it often identifies (and reports on) problems that already exist in the code that is modified by that patch. Developers have a love-and-hate relationship with the resulting reports; it is good to know about the bugs, but nobody is thrilled to be given a laundry list of problems in need of fixing, especially if it is seen as an obstacle that must be overcome before they can merge the changes they actually intended to implement.

Recently, Sashiko developer Roman Gushchin announced that pre-existing bugs would no longer be included in patch reviews; instead, they will be stored into a separate database and made available to maintainers. Ted Ts'o started the Summit discussion by mentioning this change, noting that there is a large backlog of problems that no longer appear in Sashiko reviews. Peter Zijlstra asked why this change was made, since it is still important to know about those bugs; Gushchin answered that the bugs are still being detected, but they are hidden from the reports and set aside waiting for somebody to deal with them.

Christoph Hellwig said that it would be better to keep the pre-existing problems in the patch review, but perhaps mark them differently so that submitters know they are not required to address them. Old problems should not be forced onto new submitters, he said. Mark Brown agreed, saying that submitters should not have to fix a structural problem in order to get a spelling fix in. Hellwig added that removing the pre-existing items is worse, since they are problems that need to be fixed eventually. Gushchin repeated that those problems are collected for people who are interested in fixing them.

Zijlstra complained about maintainers who refuse to consider patches until Sashiko reports that it has found no problems. Hellwig, though, said that Sashiko reviews are a good test for submitters; if they know what they are doing, they should be able to explain why a warning is wrong. Thomas Gleixner replied that the worst submitters will just feed the review into their LLM and send the response.

Ts'o suggested that the best practice will differ from one subsystem to the next. In larger subsystems, the sub-maintainers may not have the skills to tell whether a given bug report is valid or not; they are having a harder time dealing with false positives. A small subsystem with expert maintainers handles those reports better. The solution is partly cultural, he said; it is necessary to tell people that they can ignore the pre-existing problems. Dave Airlie suggested splitting the report into two emails, one with the review of the patch and a second listing the pre-existing problems.

Hellwig shifted the topic slightly by complaining that Sashiko drops most of the recipients from its replies; they should go to everybody, he said, or the thread of the discussion will be broken. David Hildenbrand was quick to disagree, though; in the memory-management subsystem, the Sashiko emails are seen as useful for maintainers, but should not be automatically sent back to the submitter or the mailing list. In a complaint that was heard a few times during the day, he said that submitters spend time submitting multiple versions of a patch set that address comments from Sashiko before human reviewers have a chance to say whether the patch makes sense at all. The Sashiko reviews tend to discourage human reviews in general. His suggestion was that Sashiko should be the last line of defense rather than the first; maintainers should say when the automated review happens.

Review practices

That led to a discussion on review practices and when human reviewers should get involved with a submission. Ts'o said that, in the ext4 filesystem, it depends on the size of the series and the reputation of the submitter. A single patch from an unknown contributor is likely to be LLM-generated crap, so it ends up low on the prioritized list of work to review. A larger series, though, requires architectural review, so he tends to look mainly at the cover letter and limit comments to the overall design.

Jiri Kosina said that deprioritizing unknown contributors is too easy of a solution; in his experience, the patches they submit are often valid. Brown said that proximity to the hardware makes a difference, and that one-off patches to low-level code often have little value. Paolo Abeni said that, in the networking subsystem, there is a complicated scheme for prioritizing reviews that depends on the contributor's reputation, how many reviews that contributor has done, and the reputation of the contributor's employer as well. Airlie warned that tracking reputations raises privacy problems and invites developers to game the system; he understands why that approach has appeal, but a better way is needed. Konstantin Ryabitsev agreed that any reputation system will be gamed.

Laurent Pinchart said that some developers are frustrated that they have to justify their work against criticisms from Sashiko while other, LLM-generated patches appear to be accepted immediately. Abeni defended the automated reviews, though, saying that the number of patches introducing bugs has dropped considerably. Zijlstra said that, while reading LLM-generated reviews "shuts my brain down", he still thought that the tool is useful; it finds real problems.

Linus speaks

At this point Torvalds, who had kept silent until now, spoke up. The problem, he said, is that we are getting increasing numbers of patches from people who do not know the kernel. By pointing the finger at LLMs, maintainers are blaming the wrong thing. LLMs can do a good job, but they are being driven by people who have no clue, and the results are ugly. He suggested maybe delaying reports from Sashiko for 24 hours; that will keep clueless developers from getting an immediate response and cranking out new versions of their work.

He has been playing with an LLM on a separate project for several months to learn about the technology, but hadn't used one on the kernel until last week. He took an old patch and had the LLM revise it; the result has made him unwilling to work without an LLM. He plans to post that series at some point. Why bother, he asked, to search for all of the call sites of a function he has just modified, when the tool can fix them all automatically? But, he said, it took six months to set up the tool to not produce bad responses. If you are a competent developer, he said, an LLM makes a great tool, but only after that effort has been put in to set it up. He resisted suggestions to post his LLM-training materials, saying that each developer needs to go through that process separately.

In conclusion, he said, arguments about how to deal with people using LLMs are misdirected; instead, developers should be focused on how to use these tools well. Hildenbrand said that Torvalds is an expert, but that we still have to deal with all of the non-experts who are submitting patches; Torvalds said to simply deprioritize them.

Sasha Levin suggested putting more information into the repository to try to direct the tools; he later posted a first installment toward building up that information. Ts'o said that it would be useful to try to provide better input for the developers who are willing to put in the effort. Alexei Starovoitov, though, answered that it is not the maintainers' job to teach humans how to work on the kernel. The community is teaching bots by way of the mailing lists, though, and that is reinforcing the problems with rapid reposting of patches.

Gushchin said that, eventually, Sashiko may be able to tell a contributor that a patch is a bad idea in general and should not be revised. Greg Kroah-Hartman said that we can expect to be on the receiving end of a firehose for the next 18 months, then we will have fixed most of the bugs and life will get better. Pinchart responded that fixing the bugs is not enough; the kernel is full of bad APIs that are easier to misuse than to use correctly. Kroah-Hartman said that the bugs will eventually be fixed, then there will be space to work on the APIs.

Ryabitsev said that problem is that contributing to the kernel has always been gated; it is too hard for many developers to do successfully. LLMs have made the creation of kernel patches easy. Pinchart said that a lot of LLM-using contributors just want to see their name in the changelog; they are not going to stay around, so any effort put into mentoring them is wasted. Torvalds repeated that maintainers should simply deprioritize those contributions. Hildenbrand asked how that could be done; Zijlstra suggested responding to a patch and seeing whether LLM-generated text comes back. Comments that do not evoke a clear human response can simply be ignored. Kroah-Hartman said that is the value of pointing out trivial items, like include files in the wrong order. Christian Brauner said that overly verbose changelogs are often a sign of LLM use.

Airlie suggested adding guidance for LLMs that prohibits them from writing changelogs. Levin has been trying to add some of that type of guidance for a while; that effort is likely to be pursued further. There was general agreement that, even if the code is written by an LLM, the accompanying changelog should be written by the human behind the work.

Private kernels

Brauner said that the work of the kernel community is changing in fundamental ways, and what it means for Linux is unclear. There is increasing interest at companies in maintaining private forks of the kernel and not bothering with upstream. Zijlstra suggested asking Google how well that works; Gleixner added that the fundamental economics around maintaining a kernel have not changed. Brauner said that the developers in the room had grown up in an environment dominated by open-source software, and that working with it could lead to a good career; if that is no longer the case, why should anybody bother contributing back?

A number of participants suggested that any company wanting to try its luck with a private fork was welcome to do so. Kroah-Hartman said that the community's goal has always been to make it cheaper to work upstream than to maintain private code. Miguel Ojeda asked whether that was still possible; he knows somebody who has used an LLM to summon forth a complete kernel written in Rust. How can working upstream be cheaper than that? Torvalds said that, if it gets to that point, the community may no longer be viable, but that is not the situation now. Starovoitov said he knew of five working kernels that had been generated in this way; Torvalds answered that there is no point in worrying about them.

Tso's said that, if somebody wants to conjure up a fast microkernel for a specific use case, they can do that now, but a general-purpose kernel designed to handle random corner cases is a different story. He thinks that there will be a demand for the Linux kernel for some time yet. Starovoitov stressed the need to understand why there is interest in generating private kernels; Linux, he said, is too full of holes that are not being fixed quickly enough, and contributing to the kernel is too hard.

Too hard, or too easy?

This was my chance to share a couple of anecdotes I had been saving for this gathering. While running on the unending treadmill that is keeping up with the linux-kernel mailing list, I had recently encountered a series of well over 100 patches, all sent within an hour from a new identity, making significant changes all over the kernel tree. When viewed that way, those patches are the obvious output of an unleashed bot and best ignored, but most maintainers only see a few of them, miss the pattern, and attempt to engage seriously with the sender. So many of those patches are applied.

There is an incredible flood of first-time contributors (nearly 800 in 7.3 alone) burying the community in patches. There have been plenty of cases of first-time contributors who make non-trivial changes to multiple subsystems over a short period of time. To me, it is clear that many of these submitters do not understand what they are doing, and they will not be around to fix or maintain those patches. But a lot of that code is getting into the kernel. My fear is that we are building up a collection of code that nobody understands, and that one day we will wake up and find that we have completely lost control of our kernel.

Airlie responded that we never really did have control over it. Torvalds said that the time has come to make it harder for new people to contribute code to the kernel. People like Kroah-Hartman have spent years trying to teach people how to contribute; it is time to stop doing that, he said. Ojeda said that there is a need to deprioritize these contributors, but that we still need to find talented people to join our community. That means finding ways to focus on the people who actually matter. For example, the people who take the time to go to conferences are much more likely to stay around. The vast majority of patches from new contributors are not worth it, but some are, and identifying them is hard.

Shuah Khan said that she can identify the people who will stick around fairly quickly, and some of the new folks who have come in are excellent. For this reason, she still spends a lot of time with people sending spelling fixes. Hellwig said that developers should be able to use whatever tools they want. For any non-trivial work, there will always be problems to fix; at that point, it is a matter of whether it is possible to have a real conversation with the contributor. Abeni, though, objected that this approach does not scale for the larger subsystems.

Generating code

Ojeda raised the topic of where generating code with LLMs makes sense. In the presence of a clear specification and a concrete problem, an LLM can create good results, he said. The tools can be good for trying out concepts; it may not even be necessary to look at the resulting code before tossing it and implementing a successful concept properly. Generating tests is another place where LLMs can do well. Brauner said that LLMs can be good for prototyping.

Hildenbrand said that the discussion had focused on new contributors, which is fine, but how can we let other maintainers know that it is acceptable to ignore patches from those people? Hellwig said that it is always acceptable to ignore patches; I replied that, for many years, we had been working hard to prevent patches from falling through the cracks.

Torvalds suggested adding an AGENTS.md file that makes generating kernel code expensive. He ends every session with a lengthy cleanup pass that takes a couple of hours to run, executing tasks like ensuring that the code matches the comments. That amount of work cannot be done with a free LLM account. I asked whether that risked cutting off bright new developers that we would rather hang onto; Torvalds answered that the kernel project only wants serious developers. That includes having access to enough resources to function in this world.

Pinchart said that he has heard from a number of developers who are afraid that they will be forced out of the community; Torvalds replied that it will always possible to write code and send email. The point is filtering out the people who cannot do that on their own. Pinchart asked Torvalds to confirm that he is not trying to push out developers who do not use LLMs; Torvalds agreed that he is not. Pinchart added that it is important to communicate that message to the community. Torvalds added that he does not want to scare off people who want to write code; his message is that you have to either put in the effort to learn, or put a lot of money into an LLM to participate.

Airlie expressed a concern that there might not be anything left for those people to write; as soon as an idea is raised, somebody can summon an implementation from an LLM. Alice Ryhl agreed that this could be a problem. Ojeda suggested setting up a way to assign a project to a developer for a period; Hellwig said that the only reason to do that would be for training purposes. Otherwise, why wait when there is an implementation ready? Ojeda said that supporting developers who do not want to use LLMs is also a good reason.

Assisted-by

Documentation/process/coding-assistants.rst was added to the 7.0 kernel to provide guidance for developers using these tools; among other things, it calls for an Assisted-by tag to be added to the changelog of patches developed with LLM assistance. The 7.3 kernel has seen 18,533 non-merge changesets (a record) added through -rc7, 1,884 (essentially 10%) of them contain Assisted-by tags. The actual number of LLM-assisted patches is surely much higher.

In the Summit, I pointed out that huge numbers of contributors are not placing those tags despite using LLMs; sometimes that is the result of ignorance of the rules, while other times contributors want to actively hide their LLM use. But few maintainers are asking contributors about LLM use and ensuring that those tags are present. As a result, Assisted-by provides no useful signal. We should either find a way to enforce the requirement, I suggested, or else just drop it entirely.

Airlie said that probably 90% of contributors are using LLMs now. Many of them, he said, fear being caught up in an anti-LLM witch hunt. He suggested adding a requirement that models read all of the kernel's documentation into an AGENTS.md file. Torvalds thought many submitters were simply ashamed to admit that they were using those tools. Gleixner suggested, not entirely seriously, adding a Not-assisted-by tag instead; Arnd Bergmann said he would find that a useful indicator of developers he should put time into helping.

Torvalds said that the tag requirement might scare people away from using LLMs, and that he would be happy to drop the requirement; Brauner added that it is pointless now. Khan said that she still found Assisted-by to be useful, but there was a strong consensus in the room that the tag had outlived any purpose it may have had, and that the requirement could be dropped.

That said, there was also a general interest in knowing how patches are created. Some would like to see which models were used, or where in the development process they were brought in. Maintainers still want to know if there is a human involved in the development process. There was agreement, though, that this information does not need to be recorded in the changelog; the suggestion came out of the discussion is that this information should be put into the cover letter instead.

Security issues and urgent fixes

The advent of LLMs has resulted in the discovery of huge numbers of security-relevant bugs, leading to a lot of fixes. Kroah-Hartman, who plays a central role in responding to those bugs, said that there are many more coming. Current tools find a lot of vulnerabilities, he said, but they are also wrong half the time; he has students working on improving that performance. He also has people and funding to help with handling the resulting fixes. We can work through this pile of problems, he said, but it will take a while. That is what the next year of work will be focused on.

Levin added that LLMs do two things that are new. One is that they are good at developing an exploit from a patch; the other is that they can be used to monitor mailing lists and figure out which fixes have security implications. The result is machines that are automatically cranking out exploits. As soon as a problem has been detected, he said, any embargo is already over. There are thousands of known exploits out there at the moment.

Kroah-Hartman said that the only thing that can be done is to get the fixes into the mainline as quickly as possible. Brauner pointed out that this leads to large pull requests after -rc1, which Torvalds has complained about. Torvalds, though, said that pull requests full of fixes are fine as long as they come from people who he can trust to have verified them to be fixes for real problems. The cost to him of doing a pull is almost zero; it's his job.

Pinchart said that, while fixing bugs is good, what is really needed is ways to prevent the addition of new bugs. Kroah-Hartman said that increased use of Rust would help in that regard; many bugs are in error paths, and Rust has better ways of handling those. There is also an effort to scale up the work on hardening C code. Some of that work is landing in the newest versions of the compilers, but developers often do not have easy access to them, and linux-next does not either. Ojeda said there is work underway to increase the availability of prebuilt toolchains that developers can use to test their code.

Kroah-Hartman said that security bugs are being immediately copied to maintainers now rather than being kept secret; it is the only way to keep up with the flood. But, he cautioned, maintainers should not be telling their employers about those bugs before they are public; otherwise the project as a whole risks running into trouble with government agencies worldwide. Once a fix has been pushed, though, the embargo ends.

Starovoitov said that there are lists of vulnerabilities that have been discovered by LLMs, many of which have reproducers available. He wondered what the best way was to get those fixes into the mainline. Torvalds said that, when there are real kernel developers involved, the most urgent patches can go directly to him, bypassing maintainers and avoiding adding to their workload. Hildenbrand worried that maintainers would lose the opportunity to review fixes in their subsystems, and that they would lose control, but Torvalds answered that reviewing all those fixes just adds stress for everybody involved.

Pain points

Prior to the Summit, Torvalds asked each of the participants to prepare a one-minute summary of their biggest pain point for the group. These presentations would happen over a half hour, with no discussion until everybody had said their piece. The "no discussion" rule held most (but not all) of the time. In the order they were presented:

  • Kroah-Hartman: subsystems that do not add CC: stable@vger.kernel.org tags to patches, but which still expect the stable team to pick up the important fixes anyway. The stable maintainers are overwhelmed as it is and are not doing that.
  • Pinchart has heard from developers that they submit code, but do not know when their patches have been applied or to which tree. More maintainers should send emails when they apply patches, he said.
  • Eric Biggers: patches that cannot easily be applied, and which do not specify a base to which they can be applied. A lot of patches cannot be properly reviewed without applying them first, he said.
  • Rafael Wysocki: insufficient feedback on patches in general. People often have opinions, but do not always share them.
  • Airlie asked his sub-maintainers; most of them identified him as the biggest pain point. More seriously, it was that the DRM subsystem is still seeing too many regressions. There is a push within the subsystem to move development to GitLab in order to use its CI features; he is not standing in the way because he wants to see how it turns out.
  • Ts'o: prioritization, and having too many things to look at. He would love to add a priority tag to patchwork that maintainers could set to help identify what needs attention first.
  • Ryhl: not knowing how long she needs to wait after sending a version of a patch before concluding that she does not need to worry about problems with it.
  • Me: huge number of patches touch the documentation directory, and it is hard to know which ones I should apply and which to leave to others. A lot of time is spent digging through linux-next to see if patches have been picked up by somebody else. Torvalds said that he often has the same problem.
  • Khan complained about the same problem; it is often not clear who will pick up test patches.
  • Hildenbrand: the high review load. Review cannot just be handed off to a bot; most changes need input from developers. Somehow, the subsystem needs to develop more experts, but it is hard to identify people who can grow in that way.
  • Bergmann said that the review process is breaking down. When he replies to patches, he tries to teach contributors how to do better, but he has become discouraged because people using LLMs do not learn. So now those who might learn are not getting the feedback they need. The opposite problem happens too: a submitter sends 50 versions (literally) of a patch and responds to comments, but Bergmann thinks that the patch should not exist in the first place.
  • Starovoitov: maintainers should not be looking for bugs or coding-style problems; bots will do that now. Instead, reviews should be strictly focused on the "why" of a patch, whether the fix is made in the right place, and is in good taste.
  • Ryabitsev: the last entry in the MAINTAINERS file, titled "THE REST", directs every patch to the linux-kernel mailing list. That generates millions of outgoing emails every day, leading to constant delivery problems. He asked whether that entry could be removed or changed to redirect patches to the patches@vger.kernel.org list. He later submitted a patch to get_maintainer.pl, causing it to only add linux-kernel if no other list has been identified as a recipient for a patch.
  • Levin: subsystems are diverging in their practices, and many now have a manual on how to interact with them. That makes life hard for developers working across subsystems; he would like to see the community come back together.
  • Hellwig: the Sashiko review workflow is broken, causing people to continually resend patches. There is no obvious answer to this problem, but something needs to improve.
  • Brown asked subsystems with tests to actually build and run them so they don't break in linux-next.
  • Kosina: there needs to be a better way to prioritize the handling of submissions. He liked Ts'o's idea, but without using patchwork.
  • Will Deacon: the process is not much fun anymore; maintainers try to explain to contributors how they could improve, but are ignored.
  • Danilo Krummrich does not mind seeing multiple versions of a series in a short time, but submitters are just feeding the Sashiko reviews into their LLM to create those new versions. A way needs to be found to encourage submitters to engage and talk to maintainers.
  • Abeni: the infrastructure is not keeping up with what the subsystem needs. Networking runs its own version of Sashiko, which produces better results, but it can't be run for every networking subsystem. That creates extra friction for maintainers.
  • Eric Dumazet announced that he had just retired, and that all of his pain points have vanished.
  • Gushchin: Sashiko has gone from an experiment to a widely used tool in the community. There is a lot of work involved beyond developing the bot itself; he has to obtain tokens to run it, explain its value to management, and more. He could really use more people and more feedback. He only knows a few kernel subsystems; if people don't tell him about problems with the others, he will never know.
  • Julia Lawall does not know if her work, much of which has involved finding and fixing bugs in the kernel, will have any relevance in the future. Five years ago she would have asked for more feedback from developers, now she isn't sure if it matters.
  • Ojeda: tree-wide refactoring work can be tricky, and the advent of more Rust code seems set to make it harder.
  • Gleixner has spent much of his time looking at design patterns and explaining them in lengthy emails. Developers used to appreciate that work, but more recently it is mostly ignored, and he wonders if it is still worth doing. In the LLM world, people say that they don't have time for design and, besides, the code works. He has spent 25 years doing cleanups, and does not want to merge more stuff that will just have to be cleaned up in the future.
  • Brauner: code removal. The time has come to fix less code and to remove more of it.

The idea was that these pain points would be discussed after the presentation, but that did not really happen. Instead, the conversation took a rather different direction.

Succession

Torvalds let it be known that he was about to go on vacation for two weeks, which is a bit awkward given that there is a merge window coming. So he was considering extending the merge window for a third week in response. There was not a lot of opposition to that idea in the room.

He went on to talk about succession plans. The 2025 Summit included a discussion on that topic that was focused mostly on what should happen if Torvalds abruptly became unable to continue leading the project. This year, he said that, while he is not going anywhere anytime soon, it is not too early to think about how a graceful transition to a Torvalds-less future might happen. To help that process along, he has started actively ignoring more emails and stepping back a bit from development questions. He is curious to see how the community will react.

There followed a wide-ranging but inconclusive discussion about the role Torvalds plays in the community and how things might operate when he finally decides to move on to something else. The two chief aspects of that role of can be described as "patch monkey" (executing pull requests) and resolution of conflicts between developers — which he professed to be bad at. In the future those roles could perhaps be split apart. The project could name a new benevolent dictator, or perhaps move to some sort of committee model.

No decisions were made — none are needed now, and none were expected. But Torvalds clearly feels that it would be better to think about these things well ahead of time rather than under pressure. There will be a mailing list created, initially with the Summit attendees as members, where the discussion can simmer. An update on Rust's project goals

By Daroc Alden
October 8, 2026
Kangrejos

Tomáš Šedovič is a program manager at the Rust Foundation. He co-leads the goals team, which helps organize the Rust project's overall goals, including making sure that the people who have agreed to work on one have the support they need. At Kangrejos 2026, he provided an overview of the Rust project's goal processes aimed at ensuring that the Rust for Linux developers were aware of how the project organizes, tracks, and amends goals. The Rust for Linux project has inspired several current project goals, so he thought that getting an inside view of the process might be helpful.

The Rust project's goals are one of several entry points to making changes in Rust, Šedovič said. If someone wants to make a change and knows how, they can just talk to the relevant team and do it. If someone wants to make a change, but they don't know where to start or who to talk to, that's where the goals team comes in. They will take a proposal, work on it in collaboration with the submitter, bring it to the right people to make the change, and follow up to make sure everything stays on track.

The initial proposal can be anything: a feature, refactoring in the compiler, test coverage, performance work, etc. Most goals are for specific features, but they don't have to be. Once the proposal is written up, someone needs to commit to doing the actual implementation work. Often, that is the proposal author, but sometimes it's another contributor to the project. Occasionally, getting someone to agree to do the implementation requires being able to fund their time. The goals team has recently started connecting people with proposals that need funding to donors who are interested in seeing that work done.

Proposals, funding, and in-progress goals are all tracked on the Rust goals web site. Some of the goals are also bundled together into "roadmaps", which are collections of related goals. Goals are supposed to be self-contained and take no more than about a year. Roadmaps are longer-term plans, such as enabling the Linux kernel to build with only stable language features. In 2024, the Rust for Linux project introduced a single Rust project goal. Since then, the ambition has expanded, and there are now thirteen listed goals as part of the Rust for Linux roadmap.

Šedovič briefly demonstrated the layout of the goals website, showing the list of goals in search of contributors, the list of goals looking for funding, and the complete list of all goals. Miguel Ojeda compared this last list to the C++ committee's list of papers, for anyone more familiar with the C/C++ development model.

Goal!

Šedovič then shared a brief progress update on all of the current Rust for Linux goals. He started with the field projections goal, which he called a good example of a well-written goal. Each goal is supposed to be assigned to a specific point of contact, in this case Benno Lossin, who is responsible for ongoing updates. Goals can also have a dedicated Zulip channel for discussion, and one assigned "champion" on each of the involved teams. The field projections goal needs changes to the language's definition, the type checker, the compiler itself, the libraries, and the operational semantics of the language; therefore it has an assigned champion from each of those teams.

The actual work on the goal itself has largely been mapping out the possible design space. Lossin promised to discuss it in his later talk, so Šedovič skipped most of the technical details. Tyler Mandry had also described some of the finalized design at RustConf the previous week.

The next goal discussed, arbitrary self types, was a bit less far-reaching. Currently, methods on Rust types can receive a reference to the value that they are called on as a built-in reference or a built-in smart pointer type such as Box or Rc. The arbitrary self types effort, which is being led by Ding Xiang Fei, seeks to extend that capability to arbitrary user-defined smart pointers. In the kernel, that could make it easier to define convenient APIs that access resources while taking a lock or using read-copy-update (RCU) protection.

Another small change is better in-place initialization. Currently, all structures in Rust are allocated on the stack, even if they are then immediately moved into the heap. That is suboptimal, since it can result in stack overflows when allocating large structures, especially in the kernel, which uses a smaller stack. While it is possible to work around in most cases by filling a slice of memory with zeros and type-casting a pointer to it, it would be nice to have built-in language support, as C++ does. Alice Ryhl is working on that goal.

The concept of an immovable type might also be something that Rust can steal from C++. All Rust types can be moved by directly moving their bytes, except for types wrapped in Pin. That wrapping has always been something of a hack, and makes many interfaces dealing with pinned structures more complicated than they have to be. The immovable types and guaranteed destructors goal encompasses the work necessary to implement a new Move auto-trait that can be opted out of to mark a type as not movable.

The const traits, full const generics, and supertrait auto-implementation goals are all intended to improve the flexibility of Rust's type system. They would, respectively, allow for using trait implementations at compile time, a limited form of dependent typing, and easier backward-compatible changes to trait hierarchies. Ojeda said that the kernel would make good use of all of them, since the ability to safely generate specialized code at compile time is generally useful in a kernel context.

The final goal that Šedovič brought up was the one that started the whole set of Rust for Linux goals: stabilizing the unstable compiler features that the kernel's build relies on. Ojeda then directed people to the tracking document where he keeps abreast of these shared goals on the kernel side, and asked Šedovič if he had any idea how long it might take to work through the existing Rust for Linux goals.

Šedovič did not think that it was possible to say for certain, but said that he's "seen a massive amount of progress" since he started working on the goals team. "Field projections, in-place initialization — those are really hard problems, but people have really been getting into it."

But it's promising, he said, that many of these language changes that were originally requested by the Rust for Linux project are now also being requested by the Python contributors who are attempting to bring Rust to CPython. It has been pleasant to be able to say "oh, we're already working on all of these things," Šedovič said. The fact that two big C projects are requesting the same features, and pooling resources to work on them, suggests that Rust will be well adapted to integrating into existing C projects once the current goals are realized, he concluded.

LLMs are usable to help with understanding code, look for bugs, review patches, and more. The policy will be reviewed regularly, he said, and could be revised in the near future.

With that out of the way, Law said that policy questions were explicitly off-topic for the rest of the session; he then turned the microphone over to Tkachov, who began by saying that LLM-based tools are best treated like a virtual assistant. They embody a lot of knowledge, have been trained on many types of material, and are able to look things up.

These tools can also write code, he said, though whether it is good code is debatable. They can write reasonable tests. LLMs are good at handling ambiguous requests, and embody a "can-do attitude". It is refreshing, he said, to have an assistant that tries out ideas rather than arguing about them. LLMs do not get bored, so one does not feel bad about giving them repetitive tasks.

He has spent some time exploring the use of LLMs to find bugs. They work best if one points them at a specific area of code and tells them what types of bugs to look for. LLMs excel at finding incorrect or missing tests, he said. The tools can create minimal reproducers for bugs and write clean bug reports. But, of course, manual review of their output is still always needed.

A related task is finding the root causes for bugs. An LLM can build compiler releases as needed and compare results with what LLVM does. It can inspect the GIMPLE and RTL intermediate representations to figure out exactly which compiler pass is broken. The result can include a proposed fix; that fix is not always correct, but it is usually a good starting point. He mentioned this bug as one where an LLM helped to quickly find the root cause.

LLMs can be good for quick prototyping and feasibility studies. For example, some optimizations can be done at either the GIMPLE or RTL level, and one usually works better than the other. If he does not want to waste time on the wrong approach, he can have an LLM try out both and produce numbers on the actual impact of the change. Once the right direction has been found, the code can be thrown away and written properly.

Other tasks that LLMs are suited for include looking for missed optimization opportunities and improving the build system. Overall, he said, LLMs have proved to be useful for GCC development, even within the constraints of the project's policy. They can assist with data gathering, running "what if" exercises, and tracking down bugs. But using them to write code would be risky even if the project's policy allowed it.

Jeremy Bennett asked if increased reliance on LLMs for many of these tasks posed a risk that the project could lose the people who actually understand the code. Tkachov answered that it is indeed a risk, but he also compared the situation to the automobile industry. Once it was necessary to understand how an engine works to drive a car, but that is no longer the case; perhaps many developers will not need such a deep level of understanding in the future. When deeper understanding is needed, he said, developers can take advantage of the fact that LLMs never get bored by explaining things to people. They can explain the code thoroughly, and help to improve understanding if one is willing to put in the time to learn. Bennett said that was fine, but that he worried about developers at the beginning of their careers who may never obtain a deep level of understanding of the code.

Mathieu Desnoyers suggested trying an LLM on a project within one's own understanding first; that is good way to learn what its limits are. Expansion into new areas can come after that. He said that one should ask the tool a lot of questions; sometimes that will push an LLM to the point where it can correct errors that it has made.

Law returned to talk about a couple of specific areas where he has found the tools to be useful. One of those is mining the assembly code produced by the compiler in search of places where code generation can be improved. He has a QEMU plugin that can identify hot blocks of code, which can then be passed to the LLM to evaluate for suboptimal code generation. This work has resulted in improvements to the code generated for popular benchmarks, in blocks he has stared at for years.

In fact, he said, improvements have been found for every benchmark he has looked at. The hardest part of the job has been in writing good prompts for the model. That has been frustrating enough that he has tried employing a second LLM to write prompts for the first one, but the result was "a miserable failure".

With regard to reviewing existing code, he said that LLMs are good in areas where the amount of needed context is relatively small. So, for example, evaluating patterns in the match.pd file is a good task for an LLM. The models do tend to produce a flood of error reports when what he really wants is a description of the types of errors it finds. They are all genuine bugs, though. Once again, he said, better prompt engineering would certainly help.

For patch review, he put together a Thunderbird plugin to do the work directly in the mail client, but he has never really been happy with the results, which are dominated by slop. The rate of useful reports was about 20%. Adding more specific guidance in the prompts clearly helps; the more of that he does, the more he ends up reproducing Sashiko. It turns out that much of what has been done with Sashiko is more generally applicable and can produce good results for GCC.

The session concluded with a summary of the places where LLMs can be useful, and a suggestion that developers should be using them to review their own patches. The community, Law said, would benefit from a way to share prompts between developers. There was a question at the end about the ethics of using proprietary LLMs for free-software development (or anything at all); Law acknowledged that valid concerns exist, but said that they were not something he could provide any answers for.

Adding kernel control-flow-integrity checking to GCC

By Jonathan Corbet
October 9, 2026
Cauldron
While many developers are struggling to keep up with the flood of vulnerability reports, others are still focused on preventing those reports from happening in the first place. Control-flow integrity (CFI) is the term for preventing (or at least detecting) exploits that divert the flow of control from its intended paths. At the 2026 GNU Tools Cauldron, Kees Cook presented his changes to the GCC compiler suite to support forward-edge CFI for the kernel.

The underlying objective of this work, he began, is to prevent a bug allowing an arbitrary write to memory from being escalated into a full code-execution vulnerability. Once upon a time, all of memory was writable by the kernel, so an attacker could easily rewrite the kernel's code directly. Over time, executable memory has been write-protected and walled off, to the point that it is all safe from such attacks now. So, instead, attackers are focused on reusing existing code in unexpected ways — calling functions with crafted arguments or, especially, hijacking indirect function calls and function returns in order to force them to jump to an unexpected location.

An indirect function call is essentially a jump to an address stored in a (writable) memory location or register; if that address can be changed, the kernel's control flow can be redirected to a location of an attacker's choosing. Preventing the hijacking of these calls is termed "forward-edge" CFI; that was the subject of Cook's work. Preventing the redirection of returns is "backward-edge" CFI, which is a task best handled by hardware, he said, and was beyond the scope of this talk.

The key to forward-edge CFI is to provide (and check) information about where an indirect call is expected to go. That allows function pointers to be validated at the time the call is made. Specifically, the objective is to ensure that any indirect call ends up at a function that was defined with the prototype that the caller expects. That differs from most hardware-assisted forward-edge CFI, which simply marks the entry points of functions. The hardware solution can prevent calling into the middle of a function, but cannot detect redirection to an entirely different function.

Checking for a matching prototype cannot prevent all misdirected function calls; a function may have a compatible prototype, but still not be intended to be called from a particular setting. Even so, checking of prototypes can drastically narrow the range of possible targets for an indirect call. Of the indirect calls in the kernel, Cook said, 55% have fewer than five possible targets. Only 7% have more than 100 possible targets. A few have many more.

Forward-edge CFI works by calculating a type ID for each function; it is a hash generated from the function prototype. Each reachable function includes that hash in its preamble, and each indirect call checks that hash before allowing the call to proceed. This sort of CFI was implemented in Clang in 2022; the kernel gained the ability to use this feature in 6.1 release. Cook said that he had been asking for an equivalent implementation in GCC for some time, but found nobody willing to implement it, so he decided to do it himself. In the process, he added, he found out why nobody wanted to take on that project.

The implementation adds the type-ID hash to each function's code, immediately before the entry point. The two's-complement inverse of that hash is stored at each call site. The code generated by the compiler for the indirect call fetches the hash from the target function and adds it to the hash stored locally; if the result is zero, then the call is allowed to proceed. Otherwise a trap is generated. There is a permissive mode that emits a warning but allows the call to proceed, and a less permissive one that panics the system.

Cook did not know the GCC code base well when he started, but he learned it over time; mapping this feature into the GCC internals required working at many levels of the compiler. The C frontend knows about attributes and types, the middle layers have to turn those types into hashes and wrap indirect calls with the checking code, and the target-specific backends have to turn those wrapped calls into the specific instructions needed. The backends also inject the type IDs into the function preambles.

At the frontend, the new -fsanitize=kcfi command-line option enables this feature. There are a couple of new attributes added to disable CFI checking for specific function calls. If, for example, a call goes into the BIOS, it will lack the necessary type ID, so the checking cannot be performed. There is also some logic to deal with inlined functions.

Type mangling is used to generate the type ID; he ended up using Clang's Itanium C++ mangler to match what Clang is doing. He found out that the Clang implementation uses a hash implementation that the compiler developers would like to get rid of, so he sent a patch to change it. The actual algorithm used does not matter and need not be cryptographically secure, he said. Now, the FNV-1a hash is used.

The middle layers needed a new RTL wrapper for function calls that includes the type ID of the target function. There was also some complexity, he said, to ensure that tail calls are handled correctly. He has added back-end support for four architectures: 32-bit and 64-bit x86, 64-bit Arm, and RISC-V.

About half of the resulting patch series is regression tests; he "broke things all kinds of ways" while developing this feature, and wrote a test for each of those ways. He has been having a hard time finding reviewers for the result; most GCC patches do not span the entire stack, so there are not many people who can look at the whole thing. Andrea Pinski, one of the GCC project's global reviewers, said that a full review was in the works, and should happen in the near future.

Cook closed with a discussion of large-language-model (LLM) use in the development of this code. The GCC AI policy generally disallows LLM-generated code; this restriction was making him nervous until he noticed that tests are explicitly excluded. Only the tests in his series were generated with LLM assistance, he said. The Sashiko review system turned out to be helpful for reviewing the work.

In 2025, that worked out to two major systemd releases (v258 and v259), and 40 point releases. The project merged 6,160 commits from 379 contributors in 2025, which is down from 7,372 in 2024. He said that the project has already received contributions from 480 people by the end of September. The systemd v258 release took about nine months of development. Boccassi said that "everybody was unhappy" with the long release cycles, so the project decided to try to do a release every quarter. "We have two months of merging features and then one month of stabilization and release candidates." He said that the project did not always hit the exact calendar date it planned for a release but it was within a week or so each time. So far there have been three major systemd releases in 2026 (v260, v261, and v262). "It's more like herding cats than release engineering, but it is working so far and everybody seems to be happy with this. So we try to keep this fixed cadence. It's better for developers, for maintainers, for distributions, and consumers as well."

Smurf village

He noted that systemd had recently codified its governance: the document is fairly brief with only six short rules as well as a list of the project's current maintainers, and its project leader (Poettering, of course). Boccassi summarized the project's governance by saying that "it pretty much works like the Smurf's village. So we have Papa Smurf in charge, and then [the maintainers] more or less are a collective, making decisions, merging PRs and so on and so forth".

Jędrzejewski-Szmek displayed graphs of the project's GitHub issues and pull requests since 2016. He explained that the rate of issues being opened was accelerating, and if it kept up the project would face a large number of unresolved open issues. "We're currently at 3,000 [open issues]. This is already too much to have a handle on. So I don't know. This is not great."

The same pattern was emerging with pull requests. He said that the project had been doing well at keeping up with those requests until the middle of 2025. It was now starting to become overwhelmed due to what he called the AI revolution. "Now we are seeing exponential growth here, and if this continues, then we will just stop reviewing and processing pull requests, and I don't think that this can go on". The pace of merging commits into the code base, however, was more or less constant. "Nothing seems to influence the pace of commits to the systemd repo much. My hypothesis is that the number of people actually reviewing things hasn't changed that much, so this is why the pace of merging is constant". If the project was going to increase capacity, it would need to change its process or increase the number of people working on reviews.

Boccassi sped through a slide related to the continuous-integration changes that systemd had made in the past year or so. He noted that the project had added more test coverage for the ARMv7 architecture, as well as build tests for RISC-V. "Not unit tests, just build tests, because emulation is still too slow. Hopefully in the future it will get better."

The project has also added the ability for a maintainer to build systemd packages based on a pull request using the Open Build Service (OBS). "This is useful in case someone opens an issue but is not a technical person, so someone else can open a PR and then can tell them, 'Hey, go and install these packages from this repository that is specific to the PR,' and then they can then test it." The project also continues to make up-to-date packages available via OBS from its main branch, or from the the latest stable release, for Arch Linux, Debian, Fedora, openSUSE, and Ubuntu.

Security

Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency (STA) awarded a commission in 2025 to the systemd project of nearly €400,000 for improving its security and infrastructure. Boccassi said that the focus of the commission was in part to help improve integration between GNOME and systemd-managed home directories, improving the systemd OS installer, as well as systemd's system-update service. He did not go into much detail about these features, but Julian Sparber did give a talk on the systemd OS installer, and Dhanuka Warusadura gave a presentation on STA-funded work toward extending systemd-homed.

The STA has also funded a security bug-bounty program through the YesWeHack platform. Boccassi said that the project had to close the program to new submissions "because we had too many submissions and the triagers just did not have time to process them". He hoped they would be reopening submissions soon. Again, the influx of reports was due to LLM-assisted bug finding; he said the results were "not great". There were 183 reports opened, and only 13 were accepted. A total of 168 were closed as invalid, duplicates of other bugs, or out of scope. Two were still under review at the time of the talk. In total, the project awarded €21,250 through the bounty program.

The systemd project had recently updated its security policy, he said, and prefers to receive security reports through a process on GitHub. He said that systemd had received 62 reports via GitHub and had published 15 advisories so far. "So the signal-to-noise ratio is better here. I guess because you don't get paid for this."

In addition, systemd officially became a CVE Numbering Authority (CNA) in June; since then, he said, it had published two CVEs and rejected one. Boccassi explained that systemd would only publish CVEs for "the really high-priority stuff". When a CVE is issued "all the downstreams have to scramble and do a lot of work", so the project would only issue CVEs when the vulnerability was considered high severity and a CVE was necessary.

Release highlights

Boccassi said that he would run through a few of the interesting features from systemd releases of the past year, though he recommended following Poettering's Mastodon account for his posts about systemd releases. With systemd v259, the project converted most of its runtime dependencies to use dlopen() instead of explicit shared-library linking, and completed the process of converting dependencies in v260. Boccassi said that systemd now only depends directly on the GNU C Library. "We have no more fixed linked-in dependencies. We still have 20-odd optional dependencies, and they are all [opened] at runtime only when a feature actually requires it." That means that distributors can compile all of the optional features with external dependencies without having a large systemd binaries by default. "So you can have a single build, fully featured, and then decide in your image if you want to have the libraries or not." This was also important for security, he said, in reference to the role that library linking played in the XZ backdoor.

Boccassi noted that De Meyer added an --empower option to the run0 command, which is an alternative to sudo, in systemd v259. That option, he explained, allows users to escalate their privileges to become "almost root, pretty much root, but not UID 0". This means that users can, for example, run processes with root's privileges, but files would be created with the user's UID and GID. He also credited De Meyer with implementing a feature that allows per-user instances of systemd-machined, which enables unprivileged users to run virtual machines and containers using systemd.

Varlink is an interprocess communication (IPC) protocol that serves as an alternative to D-Bus. LWN covered varlink in 2018, not long after it was announced. In v260, systemd added the varlink registry as a way to discover which varlink-enabled services are available on a system. Poettering described the registry in more detail on Mastodon ahead of the v260 release. Boccassi also mentioned the metrics and reports subsystem that was added in v260 as a way to extract signed reports from a system.

Systemd added systemd-imds in v261, which provides support for acquiring data from a cloud provider's instance-metadata service (IMDS). This, Boccassi said, was basically systemd's replacement for cloud-init, which has been a standard for doing initial setup for instances running in the cloud. "Nobody seems to like cloud-init, so we now have a replacement for that."

In v262, Boccassi added a feature that he had wanted for a long time: the ability to enqueue more than one job at a time. Prior to that release, if a user started multiple jobs with a command like this 

    # systemctl start a.service b.service

it would result in two EnqueueUnitJob() D-Bus IPC calls, which could lead to "a set of race conditions and nastiness". With v262, systemd now has the EnqueueUnitJobMany() API for D-Bus and varlink. "This lets you enqueue as many jobs as you want in a single IPC call, so that you can start, stop, or restart as many units as you want and guarantee that they are part of a single transaction in the manager."

Questions

Rather than taking questions during the state of systemd talk, Boccassi suggested that the audience use the maintainer round table session immediately afterward. The first question during that session was whether the maintainers had considered extending systemd to become a Kubernetes replacement and allowing it to manage not only jobs on a local system, but remote systems as well.

Poettering said that he did not think that systemd was the right component for that. "I think systemd is inherently the thing that manages the node. It's not the thing that orchestrates the node." He added that systemd-report was a way that an orchestrator could get a pretty good idea of a system's state and then make decisions based on that, but he did not think that orchestration belonged in systemd. "It's systemd's job to make this possible and provide the right tools for this. But the orchestrator—scheduling and stuff—I don't think that's systemd stuff."

Owning C types from Rust code

By Daroc Alden
October 13, 2026
Kangrejos

Rust's system of ownership and borrowed references is convenient when it can correctly model a program's needs. The Rust for Linux project has worked to provide adapters between Rust's semantics and the existing C code in the kernel. At Kangrejos 2026, Andreas Hindborg spoke about a patch series implementing the next such adaptor: an Ownable trait for representing objects that are allocated in C code, but which Rust code is responsible for cleaning up (if not necessarily deallocating) when it is done with them. He hoped to finally reach a consensus on the patch series, after months of discussion on the mailing list.

In the block subsystem, where Hindborg does most of his work, there is a memory pool set aside for handling concurrent requests. Each I/O request is represented by a bio structure. To make a new request, the block layer identifies an unused structure in the pool, fills it with the details of the request, and passes it to the appropriate driver. The driver handles the request, clears the request structure, and passes it back to the block layer to be reused. That design avoids the overhead of constantly allocating and deallocating for each request, but it also doesn't quite fit with the existing abstractions in the kernel's Rust code.

The two closest abstractions are the KBox and ARef types. The former isn't suitable for this case, because it assumes that allocations are made from within Rust code. The latter doesn't fit either, because it assumes that objects are always reference-counted, which is not always the case for I/O requests. So, Hindborg wants to add a new smart pointer called Owned that behaves conceptually like a KBox, in that it provides unique access to the pointed-to value, but that doesn't attempt to free the memory when it reaches the end of its life. Instead, an Owned pointer would call a special release() function, which could do something like release the underlying memory back to a subsystem-specific pool. Owned pointers would only be able to point at C types that implement the Ownable trait, to ensure that they are only used with types that have a meaningful release() function.

The complexity comes from making Owned integrate with the existing Rust abstractions in the kernel. For example, if a reference-counted type has a reference count of one, then in theory the Rust code holding an ARef can know that no other references exist, and that therefore it should be safe to convert to an Owned pointer. When a type can be either uniquely owned or reference counted, it also makes sense to go the other way and convert an Owned pointer into an ARef. If that operation consumed the Owned pointer, that would be sound.

The problem is that it is already permitted to use an Owned pointer to produce a built-in reference. In addition, turning a built-in reference into an ARef pointer is also allowed, since if Rust code manages to acquire a reference to a reference-counted type, the reference count must be at least one. Taken together, these features would allow for unsoundness. If an Owned pointer were used to produce to a built-in reference, that reference were used to create an ARef, and then the Owned pointer continued being used to modify the underlying item, it could result in a concurrent modification.

Some users in the kernel feel strongly about keeping the existing behavior that allows upgrading a built-in reference to an ARef pointer, Hindborg said. He thinks that they could change their APIs to take an ARef directly, and that this would be simpler, but they have been unwilling to take that approach. As a compromise, he has suggested splitting the existing AlwaysRefCounted trait (which tells the compiler that C types can be put behind an ARef) into two smaller traits. One would enable a type to be used with ARef, and the other would allow for converting built-in references to ARef pointers. Implementing the latter for a type that also implements Ownable would be forbidden.

In Hindborg's proposed Rust bindings for the block layer, there are four possible states that an I/O request can be in: owned by the block layer, owned by the driver, shared between multiple parts of the driver, and parked by the driver. When the block layer sends a request to a driver written in Rust, the bindings pass an Owned pointer to the request; marking a request as ended requires passing one back. That way, the block-layer Rust code can be sure that the driver did not retain any references internally.

He showed a diagram of the full state machine, which was made more complex by the possibility of having multiple shared pointers or relying on hardware tags inside the driver itself. Ultimately, his goal was to produce an interface that drivers could only use correctly.

Other subsystems could also make use of the Owned abstraction, he thought. IOMMU page tables and the Rust binder driver could both use it. He had discussed using it for GPUVM, before determining that it was not an appropriate fit. Alice Ryhl and Hindborg then got into a back-and-forth discussion about whether it could be made suitable for GPUVM, eventually concluding that the necessary changes could be added on after Hindborg's patch series was merged. Boqun Feng disagreed that extending it to support GPUVM was desirable. Greg Kroah-Hartman thought that the USB drivers could potentially make use of Owned as well.

One question that Hindborg had was whether it was reasonable to make the Rust wrapper type around page structures implement Ownable. It couldn't be done simply, because the Rust binder code relies on being able to upgrade a built-in reference to a page into an ARef pointer. That is necessary because the Rust binder code passes the reference to vm_insert_page(), which increments the reference count on the page. Hindborg asked Ryhl whether it would be possible to change the Rust bindings to vm_insert_page() to eliminate the need to pass a built-in reference. Ryhl didn't think so, pointing out that it is, in general, acceptable for code to increment the reference count of a page, so addressing this one case would not make it safe to have pages implement Ownable.

Kent Overstreet added that the reference counting of pages is a delicate subject, because of the interactions with the splice() system call. When prompted to elaborate, he explained that it can vary from page to page whether it is acceptable to change the reference count. Additionally, the transition to folios has complicated that code, because it is now necessary to differentiate between head and tail pages.

Gary Guo wondered why Hindborg wanted to use owned page structures at all; he thought that there was a difference between being able to edit the page structure and being able to edit the memory in the page directly, and that, unfortunately, different parts of the code had their own assumptions about what was permitted. Hindborg indicated that he was interested in modifying the data content of the page, but that he thought it was a sub-problem of being able to modify the page structure itself. Feng pointed out that the memory-management maintainers want to push developers toward using folios, and avoid exposing pages to any new users. So, the question of whether to expose pages to Rust code in this way was more of an API discussion that should include the memory-management maintainers than a standalone correctness problem that could be solved on the Rust side of things.

The patch series has been in progress since early 2025, Hindborg said, and was on its twenty-second version. The implementation has gone around in circles, with people requesting different changes. At this point, Hindborg just wants to see some consensus reached. "So please. Let's work together. We can do this."

Miguel Ojeda mentioned that in the kernel, it is often possible to do review swaps, where two people trade reviews of their work. Hindborg immediately agreed, saying: "I am absolutely for sale." Hopefully, that means that Hindborg's patch set will soon be reviewed and, potentially, merged.

The [vx]swap showdown

By Jonathan Corbet
October 8, 2026
The kernel's swap layer has undergone some significant changes over the course of the last year and a number of longstanding problems have been addressed. One problem that has not yet been solved in the mainline is the direct tie between slots in the swap cache and space in persistent swap files, which can cause highly inefficient resource use. There are two competing solutions for this problem, neither of which has, as yet, reached readiness for merging; a lengthy discussion on possible paths forward shows ongoing disagreement over the best path forward.

The kernel's zswap layer highlights the difficulties with the current design of the swap subsystem. Zswap intercepts folios that are to be pushed out to a swap file and, instead of writing them to that file, compresses their contents and stores the result in RAM. It is, essentially, a way of trading some CPU time for more free memory while keeping data close to hand and minimizing disk I/O. Use of zswap has become increasingly popular with current workloads.

The problem is that every folio stored in zswap must still have a slot assigned in a persistent swap device. If the zswap area overflows, folios may be flushed out into their assigned (persistent) slots, but normally the intent is for that to not happen. So, most of the time, that storage space is entirely wasted. It must, however, be present for zswap to be used. This problem is, it seems, especially vexing in large data centers, where massive amounts of flash storage must be dedicated to storing nothing.

One proposed solution to this problem, vswap by Nhat Pham, was described in this article. Vswap adds a new virtualization layer on top of the existing swap devices; when a folio is added to the swap cache, it is assigned a slot in this virtual layer. That slot can then be mapped to a number of different locations, including a slot in any physical swap file, a slot in zswap, or, for pages containing only zeroes, no slot at all. Folios can move between backing devices while retaining their virtual slot, making it easy to flush folios out of zswap or to evacuate a swap file prior to its removal. An xarray data structure is used to track the slots. This work has been through numerous revisions as the swap layer has changed underneath it; vswap was last posted in mid-September.

The alternative is xswap, by Baoquan He and Chris Li. In its mid-September form (it has since been updated), xswap can be thought of as a virtual swap device that does not actually have any backing store. Attempts to write folios to an xswap device do not succeed, so the device cannot actually store any data. What it can do is to provide a "physical" swap slot for folios stored in zswap. On a system where xswap is the only configured swap device, folios can be stored in zswap with no physical storage needed. Xswap uses a sparsely mapped address range for its housekeeping data, minimizing its memory use when swap usage is light.

Toward the beginning of September, Pham, who has been working on vswap for over a year, posted a lengthy query, asking how work on a virtualized swap layer could move forward. He summarized both the vswap and xswap work, noting that both projects had their advantages. For example, he said, the sparsely mapped housekeeping used by xswap was perhaps more attractive than the xarray he used for vswap.

Pham suggested that parts of both projects could eventually be merged, leading to the question of which should go first. Perhaps unsurprisingly, he suggested that vswap should be applied ahead of xswap; the latter, he suggested, can be seen mostly as an optimization that could be adopted once the major design elements of vswap were in place.

Equally unsurprisingly, the xswap developers argued that it would make more sense to merge xswap first. "The VM_SPARSE array is not an optimization to bolt on later; it is a structural choice, and the code reflects it". If vswap were to be applied first, He said, its xarray-based data structures would eventually have to be replaced with the xswap implementation. Better to add the relatively simple "foundation" that is xswap than to add the extra complexity of vswap only to remove it shortly thereafter. Kairui Song, who has done much of the swap-modernization work to date, echoed some of these arguments.

While no decisions were made in the extensive ensuing discussion, it quickly became clear that there was not a groundswell of support for xswap. The fact that xswap lacked a way to write back folios from zswap to a physical swap device was seen as a severe shortcoming that did not exist in vswap. The most contentious issue, though, stemmed from the sparsely mapped housekeeping area that is the core "structural choice" underlying xswap.

Specifically, that area requires that its full address space be reserved at the outset; that, in turn, sets a maximum size for the xswap device and, consequently, the maximum number of folios that can be stored in zswap. The size of the swap area presented by vswap, instead, can grow or shrink dynamically as needed by the workload. Johannes Weiner argued that requiring administrators to set the size of the swap area is inherently mistaken; how much memory can be stored in zswap depends heavily on how effective its compression is, which will be dependent on the workload. There is no way for an administrator to know what the proper size is, and no reason for the kernel to apply limits beyond those that already control memory use, he said.

This part of the discussion went on for a while, and it became clear that the limits applied to swap space (both in device sizing and with control groups) were being used in different and somewhat contradictory ways. Some sites use the limits to regulate the maximum virtual size of processes, while others are interested specifically in controlling the use of physical swap space. In the former case, charging zswap pages against the limits makes sense; in the latter case, it does not. Once zswap is separated from physical swap slots, developers will have to decide which use case to support, and somebody seems sure to go away unhappy. This message from Gregory Price describes the disconnect (and a possible resolution) in some detail.

The conversation eventually slowed down without any clear conclusions. An ongoing effort to set up a conference call to discuss the issue has not yet found success. In the meantime, He has posted a new xswap implementation in three parts. Part 1 is the core swap-device implementation as described above, part 2 implements the physical writeback that was missing from previous revisions, and part 3 implements the accounting for use of physical swap space. So far, the only commentary is this message from Pham, who was unhappy about the perceived use of his ideas without attribution.

The evolution of the kernel's swap subsystem has been rapid over the last year, after many years of relative stasis. The virtualization of the swap space is a logical next step that will resolve a number of longstanding problems. But the memory-management developers will want to be sure that they are getting it right; they will have to live with the consequences of their decisions for years to come. Changes in this part of the kernel often take a long time to land even in the absence of disagreement over fundamental details; this one may take longer than usual.

Graham also talked about the advantages of using Flatpaks for applications, such as the ease of managing Flatpaks. "One of the most important things is that you can fearlessly install and remove them. There's no danger that you might accidentally delete your entire operating system by installing Steam, or uninstalling it, as a purely hypothetical" example. Discover, KDE's software-management application, "presents software in a sane and coherent way". It provides a single application to install Flatpaks, add-ons for KDE Plasma, as well as operating-system updates. "Everything is coherent. Everything is good. Apps are displayed as apps. OS updates are displayed as OS updates. Things are fast to find. It all works really well."

Complaints

Users also had complaints, of course. The big one, Graham said, was that KDE Linux does not have a package manager. While he had touted the benefits of Flatpak, he admitted that the lack of a package manager was a problem for more technical users. If a developer wanted a command-line tool or "random build dependency", things became complicated. That led to losing some users who might help improve KDE Linux because they found it frustrating to try work around the lack of a package manager.

Sitter added that there were also GUI applications with "close integration with the kernel", such as VirtualBox, that were not easily packaged as Flatpaks. There was no official support for creating Flatpaks that needed out-of-tree kernel modules, he said, though he hinted that he was working on a prototype for such a scenario.

Many of the complaints seemed to center around Flatpak's limitations, in fact. Graham rattled off several problems, such as restrictive sandboxing permissions, lack of "deep integration with the host", problems with theming Flatpak applications, and other pain points. "We have to fix these problems now instead of providing an escape hatch [such as a native package manager] that lets the issues lie dormant forever." He hoped that some of the problems that KDE Linux users had would be solved by the next major version of Flatpak that is being worked on.

What didn't matter

Graham said that he and Sitter expected some aspects or limitations of KDE Linux would be important to users, but actually turned out not to be—or at least not quite as important as they expected. For example, they received little feedback about printing and printer drivers, which can be tricky for image-based systems. Sitter observed that "people don't print that much", and said he could not remember the last time he printed something at home.

Sitter thought that drivers for older NVIDIA GPUs, which require a proprietary kernel module he called "a bit questionable", would be a more important topic. However, he reasoned that there was little feedback about the lack of the older drivers because the open-source Nouveau driver was probably good enough except for people who cared about high-performance graphics. "If you care about performance in any form or fashion, then you probably run the latest [hardware] anyway". Legacy hardware eventually goes away, too: "So my hope is I can procrastinate for long enough to not have to solve this problem, even though I have a prototype for that."

KDE Linux currently uses zram for swap space. Graham said this was "primarily done out of laziness" because systemd's zram-generator makes it easy to set up zram-based swap. Zram is "like a memory doubler", which compresses memory on physical RAM and uses that as swap space. The problem is, he said, "once you're out [of memory], you're out" and then the kernel starts killing applications. "we thought this would be a big problem, but frankly, it's not that bad." The plan is, eventually, to move to zswap, but "the need hasn't been as pressing as we thought it was going to be, basically. Turns out zram is actually fine". GNOME OS, which is a similar distribution showcase for the GNOME ecosystem, moved to zswap in September.

The common trend, Graham said, was that there were several "pain points we expected to exist, but didn't [...] it turns out they're only a medium to small problem for a small number of people". Since the KDE Linux project has limited resources, it was decided "not to target these micro-optimizations right now". For now, the focus is on making KDE Linux usable for users and developers.

Architecture

Sitter perked up, declared "oh, here comes my part—the technobabble", and began to talk about the architecture of KDE Linux. The base operating system could be any distribution, he said; right now it is Arch Linux, but it could also be Fedora or the project could use Apache BuildStream to assemble the distribution. KDE Linux uses Btrfs, with snapshots turned on by default. Sitter said it was obvious why Btrfs had been chosen: "It's copy-on-write, it has snapshots, it has quotas, it has compression, it has all the things you want in a modern filesystem, right?" At this point an audience member shouted out something about performance. Sitter replied that Btrfs's performance was "perfectly fine for the average user".

The /usr directory on KDE Linux is a read-only EROFS filesystem. Sitter said that EROFS was chosen because it balances speed versus compression and size. "So it is fast and small, to a degree". He added that he thought it might be used in the future for Flatpak images and unified kernel images (UKIs). KDE Linux stores multiple EROFS /usr images for easy rollback; currently it is set to store four images to provide multiple options for users to roll back to. At All Systems Go!, he clarified that when the distribution matures it would probably fewer than that. No doubt that will be a welcome change for those with storage constraints (disk space is certainly not getting any cheaper anytime soon). The most recent EROFS images on my test KDE Linux test system are about 7.2GB each, which means that a system with four images will be allocating nearly 30GB of space in total to the current image and recovery images.

The EROFS images are created using mkosi, which is a tool to build bespoke operating-system images from Arch Linux, Debian, Fedora, or openSUSE. Sitter said that mkosi is also used to create the initrd in the UKIs. KDE Linux is using a "huge stack of systemd services", Sitter said. For example, it uses systemd-repart to create the EROFS image, and systemd-sysupdate to perform system updates. KDE Linux is also using an alternative implementation of casync (content-addressable sync), called desync, for the KDE Linux system update daemon. "It's a shim between systemd and our desync store technology for the delta updates", Sitter said, that provides a smarter download system.

What's coming?

Sitter had hinted early in the talk about replacing Arch Linux as the base of the distribution with BuildStream. He described BuildStream as being similar to the Yocto Project, which is used to create custom Linux-based systems. He said that switching to BuildStream was "currently being explored", and noted that GNOME OS is already using the project. The reason for switching was "full control over the base" operating system.

There is also an expectation, he said, that because KDE Linux currently uses Arch Linux as its base "you must be somewhat like Arch, which isn't true for KDE Linux. The two things couldn't be more separate or more different in how you use them." Not creating that expectation would be an advantage. Sitter said that the project was considering Arm and RISC-V builds, and that would be easier using BuildStream.

One thing that was not mentioned, should KDE Linux switch to using BuildStream, is how the project plans to deal with security updates and such. By basing KDE Linux on Arch Linux (or another distribution), the project inherits the security work that the distribution does for the packages that comprise the base as well. It will be interesting to see how the project intends to handle security reports, updates, and so forth once it switches.

KDE Linux is still considered alpha quality, though Graham said that he had enough confidence in it to be using it full-time: "That's what I'm presenting on right now, this alpha OS, which I have confidence in because it has OS rollbacks". He was not willing to give a projected date to reach beta, though. "I've learned not to give estimates".

He did give a more concrete answer to how far the distribution had come to reaching the beta milestone. Graham said that the project was about 85% of the way to beta quality according to the Milestone tracker on GitLab. "85% is pretty good. As everybody knows, the 80-20 principle is real. So the last 20% is ten years of effort." He hoped that it would not take ten years to get to beta, though, and asked for help in getting it there.

Questions

The first question concerned integration between the KDE Plasma desktop (which is part of the base image) and the KDE PIM applications, which are shipped as Flatpaks. The audience member said that he liked KDE Linux, but had to give up using it because the Flatpak versions of KDE PIM applications did not integrate well with Plasma. Sitter started to suggest building a custom image, but the questioner cut him off: "I don't want to build it because it takes four years."

Graham said that the right answer would be to make the PIM stack more Flatpak-friendly, "which maybe is something we could seek outside funding for". Kevin Ottens, who had given a talk the day before with Graham on securing funding for KDE to implement enterprise features, said that work was already underway to make KDE PIM applications work better when packaged as Flatpaks.

Another member of the audience wanted to know, since there was much talk about focusing on the enterprise, whether KDE Linux would have "some kind of thing that scans for outdated software" including dependencies. Sitter replied that moving to BuildStream would provide a solution to that by making it easy to generate reports on CVEs in software as well as a software bill of materials (SBOM).

The final question, as the session ran out of time, was about mobile devices. "Do you have any plans to support mobile Linux as well? Like running on a mobile phone, the kind of stuff that would usually run Android?" Sitter said there were no current plans, but it was on their minds as an aspirational goal.
